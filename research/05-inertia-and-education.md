## Summary
- **The "70% of change programmes fail" figure has no empirical basis.** Hughes (2011, *Journal of Change Management*) traced five widely cited sources (Hammer & Champy, Beer & Nohria, Bain, McKinsey, Kotter) and found no valid, reliable evidence behind them. The Hammer & Champy origin was openly described as an "unscientific estimate". A same-volume paper by Burnes & Jackson asserts the opposite, so the figure is contested rather than settled. Either way, it should not prop up the thesis.
- **Workers are not, on the whole, resisting the tool. They are adopting it faster than their employers.** Microsoft/LinkedIn (2024, n=31,000, verified): 78% of AI users bring their own AI tools to work, and 52% are reluctant to admit using it for their most important tasks. In Denmark (Humlum & Vestergaard), the barriers workers cite most are employer restrictions and a need for training, not unwillingness [search-summary]. The friction sits more with organisational permission, policy and training than with individual resistance.
- **Resistance, where it exists, looks rational.** In the 2024 Work Trend Index, 53% of AI users worry that using it on important tasks makes them look replaceable (verified). Pew (Oct 2024, n=5,273 US workers): 52% are worried and 36% hopeful [search-summary]. Upwork's much-quoted "77% say AI added to my workload" comes from a vendor survey in which the 77% applies only to the roughly 1,250 non-executive respondents (625 employees, 625 freelancers) in four countries [search-summary].
- **Manager support matters, and is weak.** In BCG's 2025 survey, frontline regular use stalled at 51%, only about a quarter of frontline staff felt they had enough leadership support, and positive sentiment rose from 15% to 55% with strong leadership support (all [search-summary], cross-sectional). Gallup names manager support as a top driver of frequent use while manager engagement fell from 27% to 22% in 2025 [search-summary]. Big tech is cutting manager layers at the same time, and I found no rigorous evidence on what that does to adoption.
- **"Teach a new way of working, not a tool" has only correlational support.** McKinsey's "workflow redesign is the attribute most linked to EBIT impact" is a self-reported, cross-sectional association from a consultancy that sells redesign (OPINION). The theory behind it is stronger: Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson (2021) on intangible complementary investment and the productivity J-curve.
- **Adoption gaps by gender survive equal access, so training and access alone do not close them.** A meta-analysis by Otis, Cranney, Delecourt & Koning finds women about 20% less likely than men to use generative AI, and the gap persists where access is equalised [search-summary]. Younger workers use AI more and in Pew are *more* hopeful, not more anxious [search-summary].
- **The biggest challenge to the thesis is about magnitude, not readiness.** In Denmark, wide adoption with employer encouragement produced average time savings of about 3% and no detectable effect on earnings or hours (Humlum & Vestergaard, NBER w33777) [search-summary]. If adoption is fast but gains are small, the binding constraint may be how much value the tool adds to typical tasks, not "business readiness".

## Key findings

### 1. Change-management evidence: the 70% myth and what is actually known
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hughes, "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?", *Journal of Change Management* 11(4): 451–464 (2011). He reviews five published sources of the 70% figure (Hammer & Champy; Beer & Nohria; a Bain article; a McKinsey article; Kotter). He traces their supporting references back to a 2002 article by Pace and Mulvin, which itself cites Beer & Nohria's HBR piece. He concludes there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence for the narrative. [search-summary] <https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/do-70-per-cent-of-all-organizational-change-initiatives-really-fa/>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hughes published a reflection in the same journal in 2022. He says he searched for evidence for the claim without success. [search-summary] <https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14697017.2022.2030980> (DOI 10.1080/14697017.2022.2030980)
- **[OPINION]** Hammer & Champy (*Reengineering the Corporation*, 1993) give the original reengineering figure as their own "unscientific estimate" that 50% to 70% of reengineering efforts fall short of the dramatic results intended. I saw this only as a quote in secondary summaries and have paraphrased it. [search-summary] <https://strategyu.co/do-70-percent-of-change-initiatives-really-fail>
- **[OPINION]** Counterpoint: Burnes & Jackson (2011, same journal volume) state that substantial evidence shows about 70% of change initiatives fail. I could not check their evidence base. [search-summary] <https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/do-70-per-cent-of-all-organizational-change-initiatives-really-fa/>
- **Implication.** The honest framing is "change often underdelivers, and nobody has a credible base rate". Any content that says "70% of transformations fail, so AI will too" rests on a figure that started as a guess about 1990s reengineering.

### 2. Adoption and training: what the better data say
**Population-level adoption (US)**
- **[EVIDENCE]** Bick, Blandin & Deming, Real-Time Population Survey (nationally representative, US adults aged 18–64). In August 2024, 39% had used generative AI. Pooling August and November 2024, 32% of workers had used it for work. NBER w32966. [search-summary] <https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/32966.html>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Their St Louis Fed update ("The State of Generative AI Adoption in 2025", 13 Nov 2025): adoption among adults rose about 10 points in a year to 54.6%. The share of all work hours spent using generative AI rose from 4.1% (Nov 2024) to 5.7% (Aug 2025). [search-summary] <https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2025/nov/state-generative-ai-adoption-2025>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Gallup quarterly workforce panel, Q1 2026 (fieldwork 4–19 Feb 2026, n=23,717 US employees). 50% use AI at work at least occasionally, up from 21% in Q2 2023. 28% use it frequently (a few times a week or more). [search-summary] <https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/half-of-all-us-employees-now-use-artificial-intelligence-at-work-crossing-landmark-threshold-for-first-time-gallup-data-shows-daily-and-weekly-usage-hitting-all-time-high-of-28-percent-in-q1-2026-with-65-percent-feeling-positive-about-its-impact-on-productivity>
- **[EVIDENCE, unverified]** Gallup Q2 2026 (May 2026), reported only in secondary coverage: about 30% frequent use and 15% daily. Employees saying their organisation has integrated AI rose from 41% to 47%. About 25% say their organisation has communicated a clear AI plan. [search-summary] <https://fortune.com/2026/01/25/ai-use-frequency-employed-adults-tech-workers-retail-manufacturing-gallup-poll/> (coverage of an earlier wave; the Q2 figures need checking at news.gallup.com)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Pew (published 25 Feb 2025; survey Oct 2024; n=5,273 employed US adults). 63% don't use AI much or at all in their job. About 16% say at least some of their work is done with AI. About one in ten use chatbots daily or a few times a week. [search-summary] <https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/>
- **Note on conflict.** "Half of workers use AI" (Gallup, any use) and "5.7% of work hours" (Bick et al.) are both credible. They measure breadth and depth. Breadth is high; intensity is low.

**Employer encouragement and training**
- **[EVIDENCE]** Humlum & Vestergaard, "The unequal adoption of ChatGPT exacerbates existing inequalities among workers", *PNAS* 122(1) e2414972121 (2025). Large Danish survey (11 exposed occupations) linked to administrative registers. Workers see real productivity potential but are held back by employer restrictions and a perceived need for training. In the IZA working-paper version (DP 16992), 43% said they needed training and 35% said their employer restricted use. These percentages are from secondary reports of the working paper and are not confirmed against PNAS. [search-summary] <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2414972121>; <https://grip.globalrelay.com/chatgpt-use-depends-on-employer-restrictions-and-training-study-finds>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Humlum & Vestergaard, "Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects" (NBER w33777, 2025; revised Oct 2025). Danish survey of 2023–24 chatbot use linked to registers. Most employers now encourage chatbot use, and firm-led investments raise adoption and create new job tasks. Average time savings are about 3%. Effects on earnings and recorded hours are precisely estimated near zero (they rule out effects larger than about 1%, or 2% in the revision). [search-summary] <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:33777>; <https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BFI_WP_2025-56-3.pdf>
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: vendor survey, n=31,000 knowledge workers, 31 markets, online, Edelman, 15 Feb–28 Mar 2024)]** Microsoft/LinkedIn Work Trend Index 2024 (8 May 2024). 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work. Only 39% of AI users have received AI training from their company. Only 25% of companies plan to offer generative AI training that year. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part>
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: consultancy survey)]** BCG, AI at Work 2025 (press release 26 June 2025). Only 36% of employees say their training is enough. 18% of regular AI users report no training at all. Secondary coverage reports that BCG found five or more hours of training linked to more regular use; I could not confirm that. [search-summary] <https://www.bcg.com/press/26june2025-beyond-ai-adoption-full-potential>; <https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/bcgs-ai-at-work-2025-report-four-takeaways-for-hr-leaders/>
- **Gap.** I found no randomised field experiment that isolates the effect of *training* on AI adoption or performance in this session. The training evidence above is correlational or self-reported.

### 3. Teaching a new way of working, not a new tool
- **[OPINION / weak EVIDENCE: consultancy survey, self-reported, cross-sectional]** McKinsey, "The state of AI: How organizations are rewiring to capture value" (March 2025). Of 25 organisational attributes tested, workflow redesign had the biggest association with self-reported EBIT impact from generative AI. Only 21% of respondents using generative AI had fundamentally redesigned at least some workflows. [search-summary; from a secondary briefing, not McKinsey's text] <https://artificialintellitools.blogspot.com/2025/04/briefing-document-state-of-ai-how.html>
- **[OPINION / weak EVIDENCE]** McKinsey, "The state of AI in 2025: Agents, innovation, and transformation" (Nov 2025). 39% of respondents attribute any EBIT impact to AI. "High performers" are nearly three times as likely to have fundamentally redesigned workflows. [search-summary] <https://www.mckinsey.com.br/en/our-insights/the-state-of-ai>
  - *Limitations:* the EBIT impact is self-reported by respondents. "High performers" are defined by that same self-report. The data are cross-sectional, so successful firms may simply redesign more (reverse causality). And McKinsey sells redesign work.
- **[EVIDENCE: peer-reviewed theory with calibration]** Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson, "The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies", *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 13(1): 333–72 (2021). General-purpose technologies need large, mostly intangible complementary investments (process redesign, training, new business models) that national accounts miss. So measured productivity is understated early and overstated later. Adjusting for hardware and software intangibles puts US TFP 15.9% above official figures by end-2017. AI-specific effects were "small but growing" at the time. [search-summary] <https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mac.20180386>; open working paper <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148>
  - This is the strongest academic support for "the transformation is the hard part". But it is a macro argument about investment lags, not evidence that any particular change method works.
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: vendor self-study, n=132 surveyed + 53 interviews, August 2025)]** Anthropic surveyed its own engineers and researchers. They self-report using Claude in about 59% of their work, with about a 50% productivity gain, up from 28% and 20% a year earlier. The gain shows up as "considerably more output volume" rather than much less time per task. Staff also reported worry about skill atrophy and fewer mentoring interactions. Anthropic is an AI vendor, and this population is the most AI-fluent there is. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic> (2 Dec 2025)
  - *Relevance:* even in an AI-native firm, the "new way of working" changes how skills are learnt and passed on (who juniors ask, what gets practised). That is an education burden this track should name.

### 4. Generational, gender and country friction
- **[EVIDENCE]** Age. In Pew (Oct 2024), workers aged 18–29 were the most likely to use chatbots at least a few times a month. Younger and higher-income workers were more likely to feel hopeful or excited. [search-summary] <https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/>
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: vendor survey)]** The Work Trend Index 2024 says bring-your-own-AI "cuts across all generations", not just Gen Z. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part>
- **Gap.** This session did not reach a source showing *younger* workers as *more anxious*. The brief's "more adoption but more anxiety" pattern is unverified here.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Gender. Otis, Cranney, Delecourt & Koning, "Global Evidence on Gender Gaps and Generative AI" (HBS/OSF working paper, Oct 2024, later revised). A meta-analysis of 16–18 studies covering more than 140,000 people finds women about 20% less likely than men to use generative AI. The gap appears in nearly all regions, sectors and occupations, and it persists in settings where access was equalised. The authors suggest ethical concerns and fear of being judged for relying on AI as possible drivers. [search-summary] <https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/global-evidence-gender-gaps-and-generative-ai>; <https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/women-are-avoiding-using-artificial-intelligence-can-that-hurt-their-careers>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Humlum & Vestergaard (PNAS 2025) also find women less likely to have used ChatGPT within the same occupation. Women more often cite training needs, while men more often cite employer restrictions and data confidentiality. [search-summary] <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2414972121>
- **[EVIDENCE (vendor platform data, one model, consumer product)]** Country. Anthropic Economic Index (15 Sep 2025), based on 1 million Claude.ai conversations, uses an AI Usage Index (usage share ÷ working-age population share). Israel scores 7, Singapore 4.57, and India 0.27. Usage "strongly correlates with income". Low-usage countries are more likely to delegate whole tasks (automation), while high-usage countries show more iteration (augmentation). This reflects one vendor's users only. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-september-2025-report>

### 5. Middle management: blocker or lever?
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: polling firm, cross-sectional)]** Gallup, *State of the Global Workplace 2026* (published around April 2026, covering 2025). Global manager engagement fell from 27% (2024) to 22% (2025), and from 31% in 2022. Gallup reports manager-led support as one of the two top drivers of frequent AI use, and says managers who champion AI raise employees' perception of AI impact "8.7-fold". I have not seen the primary text for this figure, so the exact wording and the comparison group are unverified. [search-summary] <https://www.unleash.ai/strategy-and-leadership/analysis/gallups-state-of-the-global-workplace-2026-report-three-key-decisions-for-hr-leaders>; earlier wave: <https://www.hrdive.com/news/engagement-among-managers-is-slipping-affecting-ai-use-gallup/817069/>
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: consultancy survey)]** BCG AI at Work 2025. Frontline regular use is 51%, stalled versus about 52% a year earlier. Only about a quarter of frontline staff say they get enough support from leaders. Positive sentiment toward generative AI rises from 15% to 55% with strong leadership support. Note that "frontline" in BCG's survey means frontline *white-collar* employees. [search-summary] <https://www.bcg.com/press/26june2025-beyond-ai-adoption-full-potential>; <https://idm.net.au/node/15229>
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: vendor survey, 31,000, Feb–Mar 2025)]** Work Trend Index 2025 (23 Apr 2025). "Last year, employees led the AI wave. This year, it's flipped." 67% of leaders are familiar with agents versus 40% of employees, and 79% of leaders versus 67% of employees think AI will accelerate their careers. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/2025-the-year-the-frontier-firm-is-born>
- **[OPINION / company statements]** De-layering. Microsoft's 2025 cuts were reported to target middle managers, with spans of control widening (per Business Insider; Microsoft gave no official statement at the time). Amazon talks about a "builder ratio", and Jassy's memo backed fewer layers. Google's Pichai told staff it had cut VP and manager roles by 10%. Zuckerberg described "defragmenting" Meta to reduce information latency. [search-summary] <https://www.peoplematters.in/news/performance-management/microsoft-wants-leaner-teams-middle-management-layoffs-coming-in-may-45121>; <https://www.businessinsider.nl/companies-are-culling-managers-mark-zuckerberg-explained-his-mathematical-approach-to-it-nearly-2-years-ago/>
- **Gap.** This session found no peer-reviewed or systematic study of the effects of the 2023–2025 big-tech de-layering. The evidence is executive statements and journalism, including a few employee anecdotes about more meetings and wider spans.
- **Synthesis.** The correlational data consistently say managers are the lever for frontline adoption. Firms are cutting manager layers at the same moment. Nobody has measured what that combination does to adoption. That contradiction is worth writing about.

### 6. Hiding AI use and shadow AI
- **[EVIDENCE (weak: vendor survey, n=31,000)]** Work Trend Index 2024. 78% of AI users bring their own AI tools to work (80% at small and medium companies). 52% are reluctant to admit using AI for their most important tasks. 53% worry it makes them look replaceable. (Verified: curl+grep) <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part>

- **[EVIDENCE (weak: consultancy survey)]** BCG 2025: more than half of employees say they would use AI tools even if not authorised. [search-summary] <https://www.bcg.com/press/26june2025-beyond-ai-adoption-full-potential>

## Strongest counter-evidence

**Against the thesis ("people resist a whole new way of working"):**
- Bottom-up adoption is fast and widespread. Bick et al. find adoption faster than PCs or the internet at a similar stage, and 78% of AI users bring their own tools. Workers in Denmark cite employer restrictions and lack of training, not unwillingness. The organisation, through its policy, permission, procurement and fear of data leakage, is often the brake. The employees are not.
- Hidden use (52% reluctant to admit; 69% mention stigma) means apparent "resistance" may partly be invisible adoption.

**Against "the business isn't ready, so AI fails":**
- In Denmark, broad adoption with employer encouragement still produced average time savings of only about 3% and no earnings or hours effects (Humlum & Vestergaard). Even when adoption and encouragement are present, gains for typical workers are small. Readiness may not be the binding constraint. The tool's marginal value in many ordinary jobs may be.
- Work hours spent with generative AI are only about 5.7% of all US hours (Bick et al., Aug 2025). The upside from "fixing readiness" is bounded by how little work currently touches AI.

**Against the mainstream reading of change management:**
- The "70% fail" base rate is unsupported. "Transformation is hard" may be true, but nobody has measured *how* hard in a way that supports the folklore.
- The strongest evidence that redesign matters (the J-curve) predicts delayed gains, not failure. Firms reporting "no ROI yet" may be early on the curve rather than failing.

**Against "managers are the lever":**
- The evidence (Gallup, BCG) is cross-sectional. Teams with supportive managers may differ in many ways, such as role type and resources. No causal estimate was found.

## Open questions
1. Is there a randomised or quasi-experimental study of AI *training* (not access) on adoption and performance? Candidates to check: firm-level field experiments in 2025–26, and the Danish register follow-ups.
2. What does the KPMG/University of Melbourne 2025 global trust study (about 48,000 respondents, 47 countries) actually report on concealed AI use and on use contrary to policy? Not reached this session.
3. What do Gallup's primary releases say about the "8.7-fold" manager effect, its comparison group and its wording?
4. Has anyone measured adoption or productivity after de-layering? Academic span-of-control literature, and Amazon or Meta disclosures.
5. Is there credible evidence that *younger* workers are *more* anxious about AI at work? Pew suggests the opposite.
6. What does Gartner's manager or "change fatigue" research actually measure? Not reached.
7. Does the gender gap close in firms with mandated or sanctioned tools, or only in experiments? Otis et al. say no even with equal access, but firm-level evidence is thin.

## What's genuinely uncertain
- **Causality of every "lever".** Manager support, training and workflow redesign all correlate with better outcomes in cross-sectional surveys. None has a clean causal estimate in what I found.
- **Whether small measured gains reflect a J-curve lag or a ceiling.** The Danish 3% could rise as complementary investment accumulates, or it could be the realistic level for many occupations.
- **Level of hidden use.** BYOAI estimates come from vendor surveys of online knowledge workers. True prevalence across all workers, including frontline and SMB, is unknown.
- **Effects of de-layering on adoption.** There is no data. Opinion runs in both directions.
- **Secondary-only numbers.** All figures marked [search-summary] need re-checking against the primary page. Because of the access limits, this applies to BCG, Gallup, Pew, McKinsey, Humlum & Vestergaard, Otis et al., Bick et al. and Upwork.

## Sources worth reading in full
1. Humlum & Vestergaard, "Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects", NBER w33777 (2025). The best administrative-data evidence that adoption plus encouragement still yields small gains; it directly tests the thesis. <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:33777>
2. Hughes, "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?", JCM (2011), plus the 2022 reflection. Kills the most-used change statistic. <https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/do-70-per-cent-of-all-organizational-change-initiatives-really-fa/>
3. Humlum & Vestergaard, PNAS (2025). Employer restrictions and training as barriers, and the gender gap, with register data. <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2414972121>
4. Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson, "The Productivity J-Curve" (2021). The theory behind "the transformation is the hard part", and why early ROI looks bad. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148>
5. Bick, Blandin & Deming, St Louis Fed "State of Generative AI Adoption in 2025". Representative breadth and intensity data. <https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2025/nov/state-generative-ai-adoption-2025>
6. Otis, Cranney, Delecourt & Koning, "Global Evidence on Gender Gaps and Generative AI". Shows access and training do not close adoption gaps. <https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cid/publications/global-evidence-gender-gaps-and-generative-ai>
7. Pew, "U.S. Workers Are More Worried Than Hopeful About Future AI Use in the Workplace" (Feb 2025). A probability-based read on worker sentiment by age and income. <https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/>
8. Microsoft/LinkedIn Work Trend Index 2024. The origin of the BYOAI and hidden-use numbers; read the methodology and treat it as vendor data. <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part>
9. Anthropic, "How AI is transforming work at Anthropic" (Dec 2025). Rare qualitative detail on learning and mentoring loss inside an AI-native firm; vendor self-study. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic>
10. Gallup, State of the Global Workplace 2026. Manager engagement collapse and the manager-AI link; read for the method behind "8.7-fold". (Primary at gallup.com; secondary: <https://www.unleash.ai/strategy-and-leadership/analysis/gallups-state-of-the-global-workplace-2026-report-three-key-decisions-for-hr-leaders>)
