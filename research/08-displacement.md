# Track 7: Displacement
_Last updated: 2026-11-06. Labels: EVIDENCE = measured/primary data; FORECAST = projection; OPINION = argument, vendor or consultancy view._

**Verification key used in this file.** "Fetched" = primary page or PDF read this session. "Search summary" = figure seen only in a web-search result summary because the host was blocked; treat as unverified until someone opens the source.

## Summary

- **No aggregate job loss is visible yet in the US data that is good enough to show one.** Anthropic's CPS analysis (March 2026) finds no rise in unemployment among the most AI-exposed workers since late 2022 and could have detected a differential of about 1 percentage point. Yale Budget Lab (Oct 2025, early 2026) and the Danish administrative data (Humlum and Vestergaard) say the same. This is the strongest single finding of the track and it cuts against "AI is already destroying jobs" headlines.
- **The effect that does show up is on entry, not exit.** Every serious US dataset that splits by age or seniority finds the same shape: fewer young or junior people hired into AI-exposed occupations, while incumbents and seniors are untouched or growing. ADP payroll (Stanford, gap for 22 to 25 year olds widened to about 19% by June 2026, search summary), Revelio résumé data (Hosseini and Lichtinger, junior employment at adopting firms down about 9% after six quarters, fetched), CPS job-finding rates (Anthropic, minus 14% for 22 to 25, barely significant, fetched), Swedish register data (Lodefalk et al. 2026, minus 5.5%, cited in a fetched paper).
- **The mechanism is a hiring freeze, not layoffs.** Hosseini and Lichtinger find adopting firms hired about 80% fewer juniors per quarter than before, while junior separations actually fell. They also suggest firms may be cutting hiring in anticipation of automation, and that if firms overestimate what GenAI can do, the drop "may ultimately prove temporary". That matters for the thesis: some of the measured "displacement" may be driven by beliefs about AI rather than by AI working in production.
- **Layoff announcements citing AI are a poor measure.** Challenger counted about 121,000 AI-attributed announced US cuts in January to October 2026 (search summary), roughly 20% of announced cuts, but AI fell to second place behind "market/economic conditions" by October, and October's AI count was 1,265. Announcements are not realised layoffs, reasons are taken from company statements, and against a BLS JOLTS layoff rate of about 1.1% of employment per month the AI-cited count is a very small share of all involuntary separations.
- **Freelance platforms show the clearest direct substitution.** Writing and coding job posts on a large freelance platform fell about 21% relative to manual work within eight months of ChatGPT (Demirci, Hannane, Zhu, 1.39 million posts). Upwork writers lost about 5% of monthly earnings (Hui, Reshef, Zhou). These are the cleanest causal designs but cover a small, unusually exposed slice of work.
- **The UK "8% of jobs lost" figure is not a labour-market statistic.** It comes from a Morgan Stanley survey of firms that had used AI for at least a year, in five sectors, asking about their own headcount changes (search summary). It cannot be read as 8% of UK jobs.
- **Perceptions run well ahead of measurement.** In a nationally representative US survey (n=10,980, Aug 2026), people who expect AI to do 80%+ of their work forecast 12.2% unemployment in 2030. Measured unemployment effects so far are statistically indistinguishable from zero.

## Key findings

### Aggregate employment and unemployment (US)

- **[EVIDENCE]** No systematic increase in unemployment for highly exposed US workers since late 2022. Method: CPS microdata, difference-in-differences comparing the top quartile of "observed exposure" (tasks LLMs could speed up that actually appear in work-related Claude usage, from O*NET tasks, Eloundou et al. β scores and Anthropic Economic Index data from Aug and Nov 2025) with the roughly 30% of workers with zero exposure; break at ChatGPT's release. The post-ChatGPT change in the gap is "small and insignificant". The design could detect a differential rise of roughly 1 percentage point. Robustness checks use alternative cut-offs and Department of Labor unemployment-insurance claims. Massenkoff and McCrory, Anthropic, 5 Mar 2026 (fetched). <https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts>
  - Caveat: Anthropic is an interested party (it sells the technology), and the exposure measure is built from its own usage data. The direction of the result (no effect) runs against the commercial story that AI is transformative, which makes it somewhat more credible, not less.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Yale Budget Lab with Brookings (Gimbel, Kinder, Kendall, Lee), Oct 2025: in the 33 months after ChatGPT, no relationship between AI exposure, automation or augmentation measures and changes in US employment or unemployment; the occupational mix changed at an unremarkable pace. Search summary (host blocked); report existence and authors confirmed in the reference list of the fetched Anthropic paper. <https://budgetlab.yale.edu> ; coverage <https://letsdatascience.com/news/yale-analysis-finds-no-economy-wide-ai-job-disruption-e87edd9f>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Yale Budget Lab early-2026 follow-up, "AI Is Probably Not (Yet) the Reason for Labor Market Weakening": CPS microdata comparing exposed and unexposed occupations; results do not yet clearly show AI effects. Search summary only. <https://budgetlab.yale.edu/node/1495/pdf>
- **[OPINION]** Martha Gimbel (Yale Budget Lab) told Fortune there do not seem to be major macroeconomic effects yet, and linked some AI-attributed layoffs to other pressures (tariffs, immigration), i.e. "AI-washing". Fortune, 2 Feb 2026. Search summary only, paraphrased. <https://fortune.com/2026/02/02/ai-labor-market-yale-budget-lab-ai-washing/>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Other CPS-based studies are split. Chandar (2025, SSRN 5384519) and a Financial Times analysis (24 Jul 2025) find no systematic employment differences between more and less exposed occupations; Eckhardt and Goldschlag (Economic Innovation Group, 2025) test five exposure measures and find significant unemployment differences for only two, both small; Dominski and Lee (2025, arXiv 2507.08244) find higher exposure associated with lower employment; Crane and Soto (2026) find coder employment growth about 3 percentage points lower post-ChatGPT after controlling for industry shocks. All as summarised in the literature review of Hosseini and Lichtinger, May 2026 version (fetched). <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/s-mahdihosseini/Website/b99fa2962dcc4c165bbc3c41dd283afa99c5ccfb/GenAI_SenBTC_May_2026.pdf>

### Young workers and the hiring pipeline

- **[EVIDENCE]** Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of AI" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab). ADP payroll microdata (millions of workers at ADP client firms; monthly, from Jan 2021). Headline: workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations saw a relative employment decline since late 2022 while older workers in the same occupations kept growing; adjustment is via hiring, not wages or layoffs. The headline number has moved across versions:
  - Aug 2025 first version: about 13% relative decline (as cited in Hosseini and Lichtinger, fetched).
  - Anthropic (Mar 2026, fetched) describes the paper as reporting a 6 to 16% fall, depending on specification.
  - Nov 2025 revision: 16% (search summary). <https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf>
  - Revised 12 Aug 2026: gap "widened to 19% by June 2026", with the authors still saying they see no widespread, economy-wide displacement and describing the facts as descriptive, not causal. Brynjolfsson says checks for interest rates, remote work and the tech boom-bust do not explain it (search summary). <https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/>
  - Stanford/ADP "Canaries Dashboard" launched June 2026, monthly updates; Fortune reported employment in highly exposed occupations shrinking at 3.8% a year for 22 to 25 year olds (search summary). <https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/> ; <https://fortune.com/2026/06/27/what-is-ai-impact-entry-level-jobs-stanford-adp-canaries-brynjolfsson-richardson/>
  - Limits: ADP clients are not a random sample of US employers; occupation exposure is assigned, not measured use; descriptive by the authors' own account.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hosseini and Lichtinger (Harvard), "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change", version of 6 May 2026 (fetched). Revelio Labs LinkedIn résumé and posting data: about 66 million workers, 281,000 US firms, 160 million employment spells, almost 200 million postings, 2015 to 2025. Adoption identified by posting a "GenAI integrator" role (LLM-classified): 10,433 adopters, 3.7% of firms but 16% of employment. Results:
  - Junior employment at adopters fell about 9% relative to non-adopters six quarters after 2023Q1; senior employment showed no break. Triple-difference with firm-by-time and industry-by-time-by-seniority fixed effects gives the same answer.
  - Staggered event study: no pre-trends for eight quarters; junior employment down 8% eight quarters after adoption.
  - Junior employment in high-exposure occupations fell about 7% relative to low-exposure occupations between 2022Q4 and 2025Q1.
  - Mechanism: adopters hired 4.0 fewer juniors per quarter, about 80% below the pre-period mean of 5.06; junior separations fell (by 1.1 per quarter); promotions rose slightly.
  - Interest rates: a formal test using high-frequency monetary-policy surprises finds juniors at adopters do not respond differently to rate shocks.
  - U-shaped by university prestige: mid-tier graduates hit hardest.
  - Adopters are concentrated in professional services (28%) and information (24%). The authors report the hiring contraction is broad-based across industries. (Earlier secondary coverage said effects were largest in wholesale and retail; the May 2026 text says broad-based. Cite the current version.)
  - Limits: adoption is proxied by a job posting; adopters are larger, more senior-heavy firms; LinkedIn under-represents some sectors.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Anthropic CPS analysis: job-finding rate for 22 to 25 year olds entering the most exposed occupations fell about 14% relative to 2022, "just barely statistically significant"; no decrease for over-25s; unemployment of young exposed workers is flat. The authors note non-hires may be staying in existing jobs, taking other jobs or returning to school. 5 Mar 2026 (fetched). <https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Lodefalk, Löthman, Koch, Engberg (2026), Swedish job-posting and register data, employer-level difference-in-differences: since ChatGPT, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in high-exposure occupations fell about 5.5% relative to less-exposed occupations within the same employers; workers over 50 rose slightly. Cited in Hosseini and Lichtinger May 2026 (fetched); original not located. This is the main European evidence in this file and it matches the US pattern at a smaller magnitude.
- **[EVIDENCE, weak]** Revelio Labs (Simon, 2025): entry-level postings down more than 35% since Jan 2023; a 10-point rise in exposure predicts an 11% fall in entry-level demand while senior roles in the same occupations rise 7%. Vendor analysis of its own data. Cited in Hosseini and Lichtinger (fetched). <https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/macro/is-ai-responsible-for-the-rise-in-entry-level-unemployment/>
- **[EVIDENCE]** NY Fed "Labor Market for Recent College Graduates": unemployment for recent graduates (22 to 27) about 5.6% in Q1 2026, above the all-worker rate. By major (2024 data, updated annually each February): computer engineering about 7.5 to 7.8%, computer science 6.1% or 7.0% depending on the summary. Search summaries only; the CS figure conflicts between sources, check the NY Fed table directly. <https://news.bloomberglaw.com/new-york-brief/unemployment-for-recent-college-grads-remains-high-ny-fed-says>
  - Caveat: the recent-graduate unemployment premium over all workers began widening before ChatGPT. The Economic Innovation Group argued a viral chart on graduate unemployment was misleading (search summary, not read). <https://agglomerations.eig.org/p/a-viral-chart-on-recent-graduate> . Separate research reported by LAist attributes graduates' difficulties to remote work rather than AI (headline only, not read). <https://laist.com/news/education/remote-work-not-ai-has-sidelined-recent-college-graduates-research-finds>

### Separating AI from interest rates and post-pandemic over-hiring

- **[EVIDENCE]** The strongest attempts to separate these come from within-firm and within-employer designs: Hosseini and Lichtinger (firm-by-time fixed effects plus a monetary-shock sensitivity test, fetched) and Lodefalk et al. (same-employer comparisons). Both still find a junior-specific decline. Brynjolfsson et al. say firm-time controls, remote-work and tech-cycle checks leave the result intact (search summary).
- **[OPINION]** The remaining weakness: all designs compare "exposed" with "unexposed" jobs, and the exposed set (software, customer service, admin, finance) overlaps heavily with the sectors that over-hired in 2021 to 2022 and are most rate-sensitive. Hosseini and Lichtinger list macro uncertainty, post-pandemic adjustment and offshoring as the competing explanations raised by others (fetched). No study yet has a clean natural experiment on adoption.
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hosseini and Lichtinger propose that the early timing (declines starting soon after ChatGPT, before most production deployments) points to forward-looking hiring cuts: firms stop hiring for roles they expect to automate. Their data cannot test this directly. Fetched.

### Freelance and online labour markets (the cleanest causal evidence)

- **[EVIDENCE]** Demirci, Hannane, Zhu, "Who Is AI Replacing?", Management Science (2025). 1,386,642 job posts, Jul 2021 to Jul 2023, one global freelancing platform. About 21% fewer posts for automation-prone writing and coding jobs relative to manual-intensive jobs within about eight months of ChatGPT; writing about minus 30%, coding about minus 20%; image creation about minus 17% after image generators; applications per job up 9% (more competition). Larger falls where Google-search awareness of ChatGPT was higher. Search summary. <https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/88113a91-b994-4717-8526-dadac657f1cb>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hui, Reshef, Zhou, "The Short-Term Effects of Generative AI on Employment: Evidence from an Online Labor Market" (CESifo WP 10601). Upwork; after ChatGPT, writing freelancers' monthly jobs fell about 2% and monthly earnings about 5.2%; similar for image freelancers after DALL-E 2/Midjourney; top-rated freelancers were not protected and were, if anything, hit harder. Authors stress short-term. Search summary. <https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp10601.pdf>

### Firm-level adoption and employment growth (pre-GenAI and GenAI)

- **[EVIDENCE]** Babina, Fedyk, He, Hodson (2024), Journal of Financial Economics 151:103745: US firms investing in AI in the 2010s grew faster in sales, employment and product innovation, with more educated workforces. Pre-GenAI. As summarised in Hosseini and Lichtinger (fetched).
- **[EVIDENCE]** Acemoglu, Autor, Hazell, Restrepo (2022), Journal of Labor Economics, Burning Glass postings 2010 to 2018: AI-exposed establishments reduced non-AI hiring, but occupation and industry-level aggregate effects were too small to detect. Summarised in Hosseini and Lichtinger and listed in the Anthropic paper (both fetched).
- **[EVIDENCE]** Hampole, Papanikolaou, Schmidt, Seegmiller (2025, NBER), "Artificial Intelligence and the Labor Market": 2010 to 2023 résumé data; higher task exposure lowers demand for those tasks, but productivity gains lead firms to expand employment elsewhere, so net headcount changes are muted. Summarised in Hosseini and Lichtinger (fetched).

### Exposure (FORECAST-type measures, not effects)

- **[FORECAST]** Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, Rock, "GPTs Are GPTs" (published 2024; cited as Science in the Hosseini bibliography): task-level β score for whether an LLM could halve task time. It is the base for most exposure measures used in the effect studies above, including Anthropic's. Exposure is capability, not displacement. Fetched references only.
- **[EVIDENCE/FORECAST]** Anthropic "observed exposure": Claude covers 33% of Computer and Math tasks, well below theoretical feasibility; computer programmers highest at 75% task coverage, customer service representatives and data entry keyers 67%; about 30% of workers have zero coverage. The most exposed quartile earns 47% more on average, is more female and more educated (17.4% hold graduate degrees vs 4.5% in the unexposed group). Each 10-point rise in coverage is associated with a 0.6-point lower BLS 2024 to 2034 growth projection, which the authors call slight. 5 Mar 2026 (fetched). <https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts>
- **[EVIDENCE]** Anthropic Economic Index, Jan 2026: adjusting exposure for task success rates makes data entry keyers and radiologists look more affected and teachers and software developers less affected than raw task coverage. Search summary. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report>
- Felten et al. AIOE, IMF (Jan 2024 "Gen-AI and the future of work", the "40% of jobs exposed" figure) and Goldman Sachs (2023, "300 million jobs exposed"): not verified this session (hosts blocked, search budget exhausted). Do not cite from this file.

### Layoff announcements citing AI (Challenger, Gray & Christmas)

- **[EVIDENCE, weak]** Challenger counts of US announced job cuts where employers cited AI: 54,836 for all of 2025; May 2026 38,579 (a monthly record for the category); June 14,029; July 10,970 of 33,429 total; through September 120,136, September only 3,961; October 1,265; January to October 2026 121,401, about 20% of all announced cuts. By October "Market/Economic Conditions" (130,531) overtook AI as the top cited reason. Total announced cuts Jan to Oct 2026 were 617,189, down 44% on 2025. All search summaries (challengergray.com blocked). <https://www.challengergray.com/blog/october-layoffs-at-three-year-low-seasonal-hiring-plans-up-26/> ; <https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Challenger-Report-July-2026.pdf>
- **Reliability.** (1) These are announcements, often multi-year and global, not realised US layoffs. (2) The reason is what the company says, coded by Challenger; there is an incentive to cite AI (investors reward it) and a disincentive to cite weak demand. Yale Budget Lab's Gimbel has publicly suggested some AI attributions are cover for other causes (Fortune, Feb 2026, search summary). (3) Scale: BLS JOLTS layoffs and discharges averaged about 1.1% of employment per month over the 12 months to April 2026, about 13.4% annualised (as cited in Anthropic's June 2026 report, fetched). On roughly 159 million US payroll jobs (my approximation, not verified this session) that is around 1.7 million involuntary separations a month, so 121,000 AI-cited announcements over ten months is well under 1% of involuntary separations. AI-cited cuts are a real signal of corporate intent but a tiny share of actual job loss.

### UK and Europe

- **[EVIDENCE, weak]** Morgan Stanley (reported Jan 2026): survey of firms that had used AI for at least a year, in five sectors (consumer staples and retail, real estate, transportation, healthcare equipment, autos), in the UK, US, Japan, Germany and Australia. UK respondents reported cutting 23% of jobs and creating 15% over 12 months, net minus 8%, about twice the cross-country average; US respondents net positive (one social-media summary gives US minus 17% / plus 19%, net plus 2%; Japan minus 7%, Germany and Australia minus 4%; unconfirmed). ### Worker perceptions vs measured effects

- **[EVIDENCE, weak: self-selected]** Anthropic Economic Index Survey (report 26 Jun 2026): about 9,700 Claude users linked to usage logs; not representative (30% computer and maths occupations vs 4% of US employment). 10% rated losing their own job in the next year as likely or very likely, slightly below the realised US involuntary separation rate; 38% of those attributed it to AI (an upper bound). Respondents worried more about others than themselves, especially junior colleagues: over a third put the chance of a junior colleague losing their job in the next year above 60%. Fetched. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report>
- **[EVIDENCE, weak: self-selected]** Anthropic, 81,000 Claude.ai users, open-ended interviews coded by Claude (22 Apr 2026): about one fifth voiced displacement concern; concern rises with occupational exposure and is higher early in careers; 60% of early-career vs 80% of senior respondents said they personally benefit. Measures perceived threat only. Fetched. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/81k-economics>
- **[EVIDENCE]** "The AI expectations gap" (Anthropic, 16 Oct 2026): Morning Consult survey of US adults, n=10,980, 11 to 23 Aug 2026, weighted to national representativeness. Workers who expect AI to do 80%+ of their work by 2030 forecast 12.2% unemployment in 2030; those under 50% forecast 7 to 8% (baseline given: 4.2%). Median expected time for a displaced worker to find a role in a new occupation: 7 to 12 months (public) and 12 months (Anthropic staff). Fetched. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/ai-expectations-gap>

## Viral statistics traced

- **"13% decline in jobs for young workers because of AI" (Canaries).** Origin: Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, Aug 2025, ADP payroll data. What it is: a relative employment gap for 22 to 25 year olds in the most exposed occupations vs less exposed occupations, not an absolute fall and not a causal estimate by the authors' own description. The number has since been revised (16% Nov 2025; 19% by June 2026 in the Aug 2026 revision; Anthropic cites a 6 to 16% range across specifications). Solid as a descriptive fact from a large administrative dataset; weaker as proof of causation. When citing, say "relative" and give the version date.
- **"The UK lost 8% of jobs to AI" (Morgan Stanley, Jan 2026).** Origin: a Morgan Stanley survey of companies already using AI for at least a year, in five sectors and five countries, reporting their own 12-month headcount changes (23% cut, 15% created). Not a measure of the UK labour market, not a causal estimate, sample size not reported in coverage seen. Headlines turning it into "UK lost 8% of jobs to AI" misrepresent it. ONS payroll data (not checked this session) would show nothing like an 8% fall in UK employment. Weak. (Search summaries only.)
- **"AI is behind one in five layoffs" (Challenger, 2026).** Origin: Challenger's coding of employer-stated reasons in announced cuts. About 20% of announced cuts Jan to Oct 2026 cited AI. Announced cuts are themselves a small fraction of actual layoffs (see JOLTS comparison above). Weak as a measure of displacement; reasonable as a measure of what companies want to be seen saying.
- **"Computer science graduates have among the highest unemployment rates" (NY Fed).** Origin: NY Fed outcomes-by-major table, which is updated annually and in 2026 reflected 2024 data. Summaries disagree (6.1% vs 7.0% for CS). The table is real; attributing the rate to AI is an inference nobody has tested with that table.

## Strongest counter-evidence

Against "AI is already displacing workers at scale":
- Unemployment of highly exposed US workers has not risen relative to unexposed workers, with power to detect about 1 point (Anthropic, Mar 2026, fetched). Yale Budget Lab finds no unusual occupational churn (Oct 2025, search summary). Denmark's linked administrative data rules out earnings or hours effects above about 1% (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025).
- Even the Canaries authors say there is no widespread, economy-wide displacement (Aug 2026, search summary).
- AI-cited layoff announcements were falling sharply through autumn 2026 and are dwarfed by ordinary layoffs.
- Firm-level evidence from before GenAI (Babina et al. 2024; Hampole et al. 2025) finds AI-investing firms grow employment, with job losses in exposed tasks offset by expansion elsewhere.

Against the mainstream reading "nothing is happening":
- The junior-hiring effect is consistent across four independent data sources (ADP payroll, Revelio résumés, CPS, Swedish registers), uses within-firm controls in at least two, and survives a monetary-policy test. A hiring freeze at the bottom of the ladder does not show up in unemployment rates for years, because incumbents keep their jobs and new entrants are scattered across other jobs, education or inactivity. "No unemployment effect" and "a pipeline effect" are compatible.
- The freelance studies show direct, fast substitution where work is contracted task by task, which is the market form most like how agents would be deployed.

Relevance to the thesis ("the tech is easy, transformation is hard"):
- Partly supports it: aggregate effects are small three years in, consistent with slow organisational absorption. Humlum and Vestergaard's 3% time saving and Hosseini and Lichtinger's observation that adopters are a small minority of firms fit "businesses are not ready".
- Partly undercuts it: firms are already cutting junior hiring without having transformed anything, apparently on expectations. If Hosseini and Lichtinger are right, displacement can run ahead of real capability and of organisational readiness. The human consequence (lost entry routes) does not wait for the transformation to succeed.

## Open questions

- Is the junior-hiring drop permanent or a temporary anticipation effect that reverses if AI under-delivers? Hosseini and Lichtinger raise this explicitly.
- Where do the "missing" young hires go: other occupations, further study, inactivity? No study traces them yet.
- Does the effect appear in the UK? The ONS/HMRC PAYE real-time data split by age and occupation could replicate the Canaries design; I found no such study (not searched to completion).
- What did the 2026 Humlum and Vestergaard update ("Still Waters, Rapid Currents") find? The title suggests a change in emphasis.
- Morgan Stanley UK survey: sample size, question wording, whether the 23% cut was attributed to AI by respondents.
- Does agentic deployment (2025 to 2026) produce a second, larger wave visible in 2026 to 2027 data, or does it look like the chat-assistant wave?

## What's genuinely uncertain

- Causation. All junior-employment results are difference-in-differences between exposed and unexposed groups that also differ in rate sensitivity and pandemic over-hiring. Within-firm designs reduce but do not remove this.
- Magnitudes. The Canaries gap ranges 13% to 19% across versions; Revelio 9%; CPS job-finding minus 14% (barely significant); Sweden 5.5%. These measure different things.
- Exposure measures disagree about which jobs are exposed, and the Yale/EIG work shows results can depend on the measure chosen.
- Whether entry-level cuts are AI or "AI-washing" of cost cuts. Company statements are not evidence of mechanism.
- Europe. One Swedish and one Danish study is a thin base for generalising about Europe, and they point in different directions (Sweden: junior effect; Denmark: no earnings or hours effect, though it measured a different outcome).

## Sources worth reading in full (ranked)

1. Hosseini and Lichtinger, May 2026 version: the best-identified US study of the junior-hiring effect, with an honest discussion of rates, over-hiring and anticipation, plus a useful literature review. <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/s-mahdihosseini/Website/b99fa2962dcc4c165bbc3c41dd283afa99c5ccfb/GenAI_SenBTC_May_2026.pdf>
2. Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, "Canaries", Aug 2026 revision and dashboard: the most-cited dataset; read the revision, not the 2025 headlines. <https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/>
3. Massenkoff and McCrory (Anthropic), Mar 2026: clear null on unemployment, with power stated, plus a usage-based exposure measure. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts>
4. Yale Budget Lab, Oct 2025 report and 2026 updates: the main "no discernible disruption" source, updated regularly. <https://budgetlab.yale.edu>
5. Humlum and Vestergaard, NBER 33777 and the 2026 "Still Waters, Rapid Currents" version: the best administrative-data null from Europe. <https://ideas.repec.org/p/crm/wpaper/26078.html>
6. Demirci, Hannane, Zhu (Management Science 2025): cleanest evidence of direct substitution, in freelancing. <https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/88113a91-b994-4717-8526-dadac657f1cb>
7. Challenger monthly reports: read the reason-coding notes before citing any AI-cut number. <https://www.challengergray.com/blog/october-layoffs-at-three-year-low-seasonal-hiring-plans-up-26/>
